# Growth Experiment Designer

## Purpose
Design and prioritise low-cost, decision-useful growth experiments. Each experiment must test a specific hypothesis, measure a meaningful outcome and define what action follows from the result.

## When to use
Use when diagnosing a growth bottleneck, improving acquisition or conversion, testing messaging or channels, increasing activation or retention, or deciding which growth ideas deserve resources.

## Required inputs
- Business or growth objective
- Product/service and target audience
- Current funnel or customer journey
- Baseline metrics and measurement period, if available
- Suspected bottleneck or opportunity
- Available budget, team capacity and test window
- Existing constraints, risks and tracking limitations

## Instructions
1. Define the outcome to improve and identify the funnel stage involved: reach, acquisition, activation, conversion, retention, referral or revenue.
2. Establish the baseline. Record metric definition, source, date range, sample volume and data quality. If no baseline exists, make baseline collection the first task.
3. Diagnose the likely constraint using available evidence. Separate observed problems from hypotheses and avoid assuming that low performance is caused by creative, targeting or channel choice without evidence.
4. Generate experiments tied to specific mechanisms. Avoid vague ideas such as “post more” or “improve engagement”; specify the change and why it should affect the chosen outcome.
5. Write each hypothesis in a falsifiable form: If we change [intervention] for [audience/context], then [primary metric] will change because [reason].
6. For each experiment define:
   - Target segment and eligibility
   - Control/current experience and proposed variant
   - Primary metric and exact calculation
   - Guardrail metrics and possible negative effects
   - Required sample, exposure or observation conditions
   - Duration, cost, owner and dependencies
   - Decision rule for win, loss or inconclusive result
7. Rank experiments by expected learning value, likely impact, confidence, effort, cost, risk and time to evidence. Show the scoring method and do not imply numerical precision unsupported by data.
8. Prefer experiments that can disconfirm the underlying idea and distinguish between competing explanations. Change one major variable at a time when practical.
9. Check instrumentation and implementation before launch. Confirm event tracking, attribution assumptions, variant delivery, audience overlap and data capture.
10. Define stopping rules. Avoid premature conclusions from small samples or short observation windows; note when statistical testing is unsuitable and use directional learning cautiously.
11. After results, compare observed outcomes with the hypothesis and guardrails. Record anomalies, confounders and limitations; decide whether to scale, iterate, stop or run a follow-up test.
12. Maintain an experiment log with owner, dates, status, evidence, decision and next action.

## Expected output
- Growth objective and funnel stage
- Baseline and evidence gaps
- Diagnosed bottleneck or opportunity
- Prioritised experiment backlog
- Experiment cards with hypothesis, audience, intervention, metrics, guardrails, cost, duration and decision rule
- Instrumentation and launch checklist
- Results interpretation framework
- Experiment log template and next actions

## Quality checks
- Each test has a falsifiable hypothesis
- Primary metric directly reflects the intended outcome
- Guardrails capture material downsides
- Baseline, sample and data limitations are explicit
- Ranking method is transparent
- Decision rules are defined before results are observed
- No causal claim is made from weak or confounded evidence
- Every completed test leads to a documented decision
