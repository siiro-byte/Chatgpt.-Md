# Workflow Simplifier

## Purpose
Analyse a recurring workflow and recommend the smallest safe improvements that reduce delay, errors, effort or hand-offs. Distinguish process redesign from automation and protect necessary controls.

## When to use
Use when improving administrative processes, customer service, content production, operations, approvals, reporting, team hand-offs or repetitive digital tasks.

## Required inputs
- Workflow objective and intended users
- Current process description, diagram or examples
- Trigger, inputs, outputs and frequency
- People/roles and systems involved
- Typical time, cost, error and delay data, if available
- Exceptions, compliance and approval requirements
- Desired outcome and implementation constraints

## Instructions
1. Define the workflow's purpose, customer/user and desired output. Establish what “better” means: faster, cheaper, more reliable, easier to use or more controlled.
2. Map the current process from trigger to completion. Record steps, decisions, hand-offs, systems, wait states, rework and exceptions.
3. Separate observed process facts from assumptions. Use timestamps, samples or examples where available; do not invent cycle times or error rates.
4. Identify waste and friction: duplicate entry, unnecessary approvals, unclear ownership, avoidable waiting, repeated checks, manual copying, poor information quality and failure loops.
5. Locate root causes rather than treating symptoms. Ask why delays or errors occur and identify upstream dependencies.
6. Classify each improvement as eliminate, simplify, standardise, clarify ownership, change sequence, add a control or automate. Do not automate a broken process without first simplifying it.
7. Prioritise changes by user impact, effort, cost, risk, reversibility and expected benefit. Prefer small changes that can be tested before major system work.
8. For automation candidates, define trigger, inputs, rules, outputs, exception handling, human review, permissions, audit trail and fallback. Identify tasks that should remain human-controlled.
9. Assess risks including data privacy, security, compliance, accessibility, service disruption and loss of human judgement. Preserve required approvals and segregation of duties.
10. Design a future-state workflow with clear roles, decision points, hand-offs and exception paths.
11. Define measurable success indicators and a baseline. Examples include cycle time, completion rate, rework, error rate, customer effort and staff time; use only metrics that can be measured.
12. Propose a pilot, rollback plan and review period. State what evidence would justify scaling, revising or abandoning the change.

## Expected output
- Workflow objective and scope
- Current-state process map
- Friction points and likely root causes
- Improvement opportunities by type
- Prioritised recommendations
- Future-state process map
- Automation specification where appropriate
- Controls, risks and exception handling
- Baseline, success measures and pilot plan
- Rollback and next steps

## Quality checks
- Current process is mapped before proposing changes
- Evidence and assumptions are separated
- Root causes are distinguished from symptoms
- Process simplification precedes automation
- Controls and human review are retained where needed
- Benefits are measurable and realistic
- Pilot and rollback are defined
- Recommendations fit available capacity and constraints
